Situational How would you handle a disagreement with a senior engineer about design approach? ▼
"If you disagreed with a senior engineer on a design choice, what would you do?"

I would start by making sure I fully understand their reasoning — not to concede, but to ensure I'm not missing context that justifies the choice. Engineers often carry institutional knowledge that isn't visible in a spec document. I'd ask clarifying questions and listen carefully before forming a response.

If I still believed my approach had merit after that, I'd document a brief technical comparison: concrete trade-offs, test data if available, and the specific scenario where my approach would outperform. I'd present this in a one-on-one conversation, framing it as "here's what I observed" rather than "you're wrong." Engineers respond well to data. If after that discussion the senior engineer still chose their approach, I would commit to it fully and help execute it well — disagreement during design is healthy, but fractured execution is damaging.

During my MelRok internship, I suggested a different polling strategy than what was initially configured. I ran a side-by-side comparison showing a 12% reduction in gateway load. The lead engineer adopted it after reviewing the data. That experience taught me the value of speaking with evidence, not just opinion.

Situational A critical sensor in your deployed system starts returning erratic data mid-operation ▼
"You're on a live deployment. A sensor suddenly starts returning erratic values. What do you do?"

First, I would determine whether the erratic data is a hardware failure, a communication fault, or a software parsing issue — they require completely different fixes. I'd check the physical connection and power supply, then verify the protocol register read against the device's documentation to rule out a configuration drift.

If the cause isn't immediately clear, I'd isolate the sensor from the control loop to prevent downstream damage — substituting a safe default value or entering a hold-last-valid state — and escalate immediately to the lead engineer while documenting my observations. I would not attempt a live fix without informing the team, especially in an industrial context where sensor data may be driving actuators.

This mirrors what I did during my internship when a Modbus meter began returning zero-value reads intermittently. I inserted a NaN-detection step in the pipeline, replaced outputs with the last valid reading, and flagged the anomaly in the dashboard — buying time to diagnose the root cause without disrupting the client's monitoring view.

Technical Explain Pulse Amplitude Modulation and a practical application ▼
"Can you explain Pulse Amplitude Modulation and describe where it's used practically?"

Pulse Amplitude Modulation, or PAM, is a signaling scheme where information is encoded in the amplitude of a series of discrete pulses sampled at regular intervals. In PAM-2, you have two amplitude levels (essentially binary); PAM-4 uses four distinct amplitude levels, allowing two bits of information per pulse — doubling the data throughput without increasing the symbol rate.

A practical and very current example is Ethernet. IEEE 802.3 100GBASE-T uses PAM-4 to achieve 100 Gbps over twisted-pair copper by transmitting four amplitude levels per symbol. This is also how modern DDR5 memory interfaces increase bandwidth without a proportional increase in clock frequency. In the embedded world, PAM is relevant when you're designing DAC-based analog output stages — for example, in a multi-level motor drive or a precision sensor excitation circuit, where you want to represent a range of analog voltages using a digital pulse sequence before reconstruction by a low-pass filter.

The key trade-off with higher-order PAM is noise margin: more amplitude levels means the gap between adjacent levels shrinks, making the system more susceptible to signal degradation. That's why PAM-4 implementations require careful SNR management and typically pair with forward error correction at the physical layer.

Part 3 — Reflection Paper

From CV Draft to Career Confidence

~500 words · Michel Salame · AUST Career Simulation Project

When I first sat down to write my CV, I made the same mistake most engineering students make: I described what I did rather than what I achieved. My initial draft listed responsibilities — "maintained lab equipment," "wrote Python scripts" — without quantifying their impact or using the action-verb-driven language that makes a CV memorable to a recruiter scanning it in under ten seconds. That gap between describing activity and demonstrating value turned out to be the most challenging part of this entire project.

"The hardest sentence I wrote in this project was not a technical explanation — it was proving that my work mattered in measurable terms."

Translating my MelRok internship experience into CV language forced me to think rigorously about outcomes. I had developed Python pipelines and built Plotly dashboards — but so what? I had to ask myself: by how much did this reduce manual processing time? What specific problem did the sensor validation work solve, and what would have gone wrong without it? This discipline of backward-reasoning from impact to description is a skill I did not fully possess before this project. Using Claude to review draft bullet points and flag vague phrasing was genuinely useful here — it functioned like a tough-but-fair first reader, pushing me to replace "worked on" with "engineered" and "helped" with "led."

The mock interview preparation phase surfaced a different kind of challenge: pacing and authenticity. When I first ran a simulation using a ChatGPT-based interview prompt, I discovered that my written answers — which read clearly on paper — sounded rehearsed and robotic when spoken aloud. The AI simulator's feedback identified specific moments where I rushed through the Result component of my STAR responses, undermining the very part that should land strongest. Slowing down, pausing after stating the outcome, and letting the result breathe — these are micro-skills I would not have identified without the simulation.

Tone was another dimension I underestimated. My first run of the "Tell me about yourself" response was technically accurate but overly modest. The AI feedback pointed out that I was framing my internship as if I were lucky to have contributed, rather than confidently claiming ownership of the problems I solved. That shift from passive to active voice in speech is subtle but significant — it is the difference between a candidate who was present and a candidate who led.

Looking ahead to the AUST Job Fair, this project has given me something more valuable than polished documents: it has given me a mental model for self-presentation. I now understand that a CV is not a record of where I have been, but an argument for where I should go next. Every line must earn its place by demonstrating relevance to the reader's needs. And every interview answer, however technically sophisticated, must ultimately tell a story — one where I am an engineer who identifies problems, takes deliberate action, and creates measurable change. I leave this project not just more prepared for engineering recruitment, but more aware of my own professional narrative.
