First, I isolate — check thermal sensors, identify which core or component is the hotspot. If workload is the cause, I'd throttle or migrate traffic before touching hardware. I'd review recent deployments for runaway processes. Simultaneously, I'd alert the on-call team so they can prepare for failover. Only after stabilizing the immediate situation would I do a root-cause analysis — whether it's cooling failure, a firmware bug, or a traffic spike — and document it for post-mortem. The instinct is always: protect the service first, understand second.

Raise it immediately and clearly — not as an alarm, but as a structured risk report: what the flaw is, what scenarios trigger it, and what the failure mode looks like. Then I'd propose options: delay and fix, ship with a workaround, or accept the risk with a mitigation plan. The decision belongs to the team and stakeholders, but my job is to make sure no one ships blind. Silence is the only unacceptable answer.

Modbus is a simple, serial, request-response protocol — it's lightweight, deterministic, and nearly universal in industrial devices. You use it when you need raw data fast over RS-485 or TCP, and your system topology is master-slave. BACnet is a richer, object-oriented protocol designed for building automation — it supports device discovery, alarm management, scheduling, and peer-to-peer communication. I'd choose Modbus for simple sensor polling in a constrained environment; BACnet for a large HVAC or energy management system where interoperability across vendors and structured data objects matter. At MelRok, we often bridged both — Modbus devices feeding into a BACnet/IP gateway.

Because the most interesting problems in computation today are not software problems — they're physics problems. How do you move a petabyte in 100 milliseconds? How do you build a tensor processor that runs a neural net faster than the human brain processes a thought? Google's custom silicon — TPUs, the Titan security chips, Jupiter network fabric — represents the state of the art in co-designing hardware and software. My background is precisely at that boundary: I've written the firmware and the control algorithm, I've read the datasheet and debugged the board. I want to work at scale, on problems that matter at the infrastructure layer, where every watt and every nanosecond counts.

Chapter 01

The hardest part wasn't the code.

Building this portfolio forced me to confront a gap I hadn't noticed before: the distance between doing engineering and explaining it. I know how a PID controller converges. I know why we tune the derivative gain conservatively on noisy sensor data. What I didn't know was how to compress that knowledge into two sentences that make a recruiter understand why I'm the right person for the job.

The most challenging moment came when writing my MelRok experience. I had the raw facts — Python pipelines, Modbus gateways, time-series data — but every draft read like a system specification rather than a professional narrative. AI helped me identify the pivot: it wasn't what I built, it was what it solved. Shifting to outcome-oriented language — "reducing manual effort across multi-device datasets" — changed the entire register of the writing.

"Recruiters care about 'why it matters,' not 'how it works.' As an engineer, I had to learn to speak both languages."
Chapter 02

What the mock interview revealed.

Using AI as an interview simulator was unexpectedly confrontational. The questions were standard. My first answers were not. I defaulted to lecture mode — explaining the technical system in full before getting to the human dimension of what happened. The STAR method forced me to reverse that instinct: start with the situation, earn the technical detail.

I recorded my behavioral answer three times. The first two I watched back and recognized something I'd never noticed: when I'm explaining something I care about, I become very still. Not confident-still — rigid-still. My voice stays level but my body goes quiet in a way that reads as uncertainty. By the third take, I'd consciously loosened my posture and let the energy of the answer animate my delivery. A small adjustment with a significant effect.

The AI feedback on tone was blunt: my responses were technically precise but emotionally flat. I learned that communication is not just content transfer — it's an act of presence.

Chapter 03

What I know about myself now.

My strength is boundary-crossing. I'm most useful — and most alive — when a problem requires understanding both the hardware abstraction layer and the application layer. The PID project wasn't interesting because it controlled a ball. It was interesting because the control algorithm had to coexist with a computer vision pipeline running on a general-purpose OS, with all the scheduling unpredictability that implies. Making that work required thinking simultaneously at three levels of abstraction.

That is also what draws me to companies like Google. The problems at the infrastructure layer are not purely software problems or purely hardware problems. They are systems problems — and I am, fundamentally, a systems thinker.

Chapter 04

Applying this to real applications.

This simulation has changed how I will approach every future application. I will write for the reader, not for myself. I will quantify outcomes before I describe processes. I will practice answers until the STAR structure is invisible — until the story just sounds like a story.
